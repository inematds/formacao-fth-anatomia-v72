Lesson 6 · 15 min

Balance: center of mass and support polygon

A standing humanoid is an inverted pendulum on two small feet. In this lesson you calculate the G1's center of mass, support polygon, and center of pressure from the model itself, push the robot with different forces, and separate three things that usually get mixed up: leaving the support, swaying, and falling.

Start the lesson

In this lesson you will

  1. 1Center of mass, support polygon, and center of pressure (ZMP on flat ground)
  2. 2CoM margin in centimeters, every 0.25 s
  3. 3Why 25 N leaves the support and comes back, and 35 N knocks it down
  4. 4Where classical control (ZMP) and RL come in

Goal: push the G1 and say, from the simulation, when the center of mass leaves the support polygon and whether the robot falls.

01 · Four quantities, one question: does it fall?

Click each block.

Click each block in the diagram to see what it does.

Standing still, it is enough for the CoM to be inside the support. In motion, the CoP and the speed are what matter.

02 · What the push sweep shows

A 0.2 s forward push on the torso, with the G1 just holding the pose. Click each band.

1. Up to 15 N

The CoM never leaves the polygon: the margin never goes negative at any moment.

That is what any rigid pose can handle: the impulse is small (3 N·s).

2. 20 to 30 N

The robot rises onto its toes, the polygon becomes a line, and the CoM is outside it for a moment. Even so, it comes back and ends up standing.

Leaving the support is not falling: it is starting to rotate.

3. 35 to 40 N

At 40 N the CoM leaves at t = 0.73 s; the robot sways forward, comes back, overshoots, and falls backward, without moving a foot.

A balance controller would take a step or move the hip and ankle. Here there is no controller: it is the limit of the body alone.

4. Classical × RL

Classical controllers plan the ZMP to stay inside the support (centroidal models, MPC). Humanoid locomotion in research today is trained with RL in thousands of parallel simulations (course 1).

The training's research found, as of 10/2026, no independent comparison saying which one is better: the two coexist.

03 · Pushing the G1

The equilibrio.py applies the force on the torso at t = 0.5 s and prints, every 0.25 s, the CoM margin (positive = inside the support), the CoP forward, and the pelvis height.

Terminal · 40 N push
F=${FTH:-$HOME/fth}; cd $F/anatomia && . $F/.venv/bin/activate
python3 kit/equilibrio.py --empurrao 40
Terminal · from 10 N to 40 N
F=${FTH:-$HOME/fth}; cd $F/anatomia && . $F/.venv/bin/activate
for n in 10 15 20 25 30 35 40; do
  echo "$n N → $(python3 kit/equilibrio.py --empurrao $n | tail -2 | cut -d'(' -f1 | tr '\n' ' ')"
done

From 20 to 30 N the CoM leaves the support and the robot does not fall. Anyone who measures balance only by the CoM position gets it wrong from 20 to 30 N; you also need to look at the speed and what happens afterward.

04 · Practice

Find the limit of the body without a controller.

E3 · Impulse, not force

5 min

Done when: --empurrao 40 --duracao 0.1 ends with queda: não and --empurrao 80 --duracao 0.1 with queda: sim; you wrote down the three impulses (4, 8 and 8 N·s).

06 · In three sentences

QuantitiesCoM, support polygon and CoP come out of the model at every step.
DynamicsLeaving the support is rotating; falling is rotating too fast to come back.
LimitWithout a controller, the G1 takes 15 N for 0.2 s and falls with 35 N.
